# Measuring a system

Before you can scale a system, you need words for what "good" means. Engineers describe a running service with a small set of measurements. Each one answers a different question, and mixing them up leads to the wrong fixes.

## Load: throughput

**Throughput** is how much work the system completes per unit of time, such as requests per second or messages per second. It tells you how busy the system is and how close it is to its limits.

**Scalability** is the ability to handle more load by adding resources, without an unacceptable loss of latency, throughput, or reliability. A system that needs ten times the hardware for twice the traffic does not scale well.

## Speed: latency and percentiles

**Latency** is the time one operation takes from the caller's point of view. Averages hide the painful part, so latency is reported as **percentiles**:

- `p50` is the median: half of requests are faster.
- `p99` means 99% of requests are faster, and 1% are slower.

If one request in a hundred takes five seconds, the average can still look fine while real users wait. The slow tail is often where problems start, so percentiles describe user experience far better than averages.

## Trust: availability, reliability, durability

These three sound similar but measure different promises:

- **Availability** is the fraction of time or requests for which the service can do its job.
- **Reliability** is producing correct results consistently over time, including during expected failures.
- **Durability** is the promise that data, once acknowledged, stays stored despite failures.

A service can be available but unreliable, for example when it answers quickly with wrong data. It can also be durable but unavailable: the data is safe, but nobody can reach it.

## Targets: SLOs and error budgets

A **service-level objective (SLO)** turns a measurement into a target, such as "99.9% of requests succeed in under 300 ms over 30 days".

The gap between perfect and the target is the **error budget**. With a 99.9% SLO, the budget is 0.1% of requests. Teams spend it on risky changes. When the budget runs low, they slow down releases and invest in reliability.

## Planning ahead

**Capacity planning** estimates the resources a system will need from expected load, growth, and safety margins, so it meets its targets _before_ it saturates. Good planning finds the first bottleneck, whether CPU, memory, connections, or a downstream dependency, while there is still time to act.
