# Reliability and availability

A fast system is useless if it is down, returns wrong answers, or forgets data it promised to keep. This lesson separates three qualities that people often lump together, shows how teams turn them into measurable targets, and introduces the basic techniques for surviving component failures.

## Three different promises

**Availability** is the fraction of time, or of requests, for which a service can successfully do its job. It is often written as a number of nines: 99.9% availability allows roughly 43 minutes of failure in a 30-day month, while 99.99% allows about 4 minutes. Many teams measure it per request (successful requests divided by total requests) rather than by clock time, because a service can be "up" while failing half of its calls.

**Reliability** is broader. It is the ability to produce correct results consistently over time, including while facing expected failures and expected load. A service that answers every request but sometimes returns someone else's data is available and unreliable.

**Durability** is about data. It is the guarantee that once the system acknowledges a write, that data stays stored despite failures within the system's stated **fault model**. The fault model matters: a database may promise to survive the loss of one disk or one data center, but not the simultaneous loss of every copy. Durability is usually achieved by writing to persistent storage and keeping several copies before saying "saved."

## Turning goals into numbers

Vague goals such as "the service should be reliable" cannot guide decisions. Teams therefore define a **service-level indicator**, a measurable signal such as the proportion of requests that succeed within 300 ms, and set a target for it. That target is a **service-level objective (SLO)**. A typical SLO reads: 99.9% of requests succeed in under 300 ms, measured over a rolling 30 days.

An SLO below 100% is deliberate. Perfect reliability is impossibly expensive, and users rarely notice the difference between 99.99% and 100%. The gap between the target and perfection is the **error budget**: the amount of unreliability the SLO allows. With a 99.9% target, 0.1% of requests may fail.

The error budget turns reliability into a shared currency between product and operations work:

- While budget remains, teams can **spend it** on risky changes, experiments, and fast releases.
- When incidents burn through the budget, teams **slow releases** and prioritize reliability work until the service is back within its objective.

## Single points of failure

A **single point of failure** is one component whose failure can make the whole service unavailable. It might be a lone database server, one network switch, a single configuration service, or one person who holds the only credentials. Finding these is one of the first steps in reviewing a design.

The two standard remedies work together.

**Redundancy** means keeping more than one component able to perform the same critical function, so that losing one does not necessarily stop the service. Examples include several application instances, database replicas, and multiple network paths.

**Failover** is the act of switching traffic or responsibility from a failed component to a healthy replacement. It can be automatic, such as a load balancer removing a dead instance or a database promoting a replica, or manual, such as an operator redirecting traffic. Redundancy without a working failover path only gives you spare parts sitting idle.

## Fault tolerance

**Fault tolerance** is the ability to keep providing acceptable service while some components have failed. Redundancy and failover are the building blocks, but fault tolerance is the property you actually want. Note the word "acceptable": a fault-tolerant system may run slower or with less spare capacity during a failure, as long as it still meets its obligations.

## Graceful degradation

Sometimes you cannot keep everything running, whether because a dependency is down or because demand exceeds capacity. **Graceful degradation** keeps the core functionality available while disabling or reducing nonessential features.

Consider a video site under heavy load. It might:

1. Stop generating personalized recommendations and show a cached popular list instead.
2. Lower the default video quality.
3. Temporarily hide comment counts.

Users can still find and watch videos, which is the part they came for. The alternative, where every feature keeps trying and the whole site collapses, is far worse. Designing for graceful degradation means deciding in advance which features are core and which can be switched off.
