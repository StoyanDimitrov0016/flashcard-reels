# Operating in production

A design is only finished once it runs, and running systems need to be understood, sized, and protected against disasters. This last lesson covers how teams see inside a live system, how they plan and adjust capacity, how they prepare for losing an entire region, and why every design should begin with the requirements rather than the technology.

## Observability

**Observability** is the ability to infer what is happening inside a system from what it outputs. When something goes wrong in a way nobody predicted, an observable system lets you ask new questions and find the answer without shipping new code. Its main signals are metrics, logs, traces, and profiles.

A **metric** is a numeric measurement recorded over time, a time series. Request rate, error count, queue depth, CPU usage, and latency distributions are typical metrics. They are cheap to store and fast to query, which makes them ideal for dashboards and alerts, but they summarize rather than explain individual events.

Logs record individual events. **Structured logging** writes them as machine-readable fields instead of only free-form text:

```json
{ "level": "error", "event": "payment_failed", "orderId": "A-1842", "durationMs": 912 }
```

Compared with a sentence such as "payment failed for order A-1842 after 912ms," structured fields can be filtered, aggregated, and correlated reliably, without fragile text parsing.

**Distributed tracing** follows one logical request across service boundaries. Each unit of work, such as an incoming request or a database query, is recorded as a **span** with a start time and duration. Spans share a trace ID and point to their parent, so a tracing tool can draw the whole path of a request and show exactly which service or call made it slow.

A **correlation ID** is the simpler version of the same idea. An identifier is generated at the start of a request or workflow and propagated through every related call, message, and log line. Searching for that ID connects all the logs and events from one request, even across queues and asynchronous steps.

## Monitoring and alerting

**Monitoring** records and visualizes system behavior: collecting metrics, building dashboards, and keeping history. **Alerting** is a separate step that notifies people or automation when selected conditions require action. You monitor far more than you alert on.

An alert is **actionable** when it signals a condition with real user or operational impact and points toward an owner or a response. "Checkout error rate above SLO for 10 minutes" is actionable. "CPU briefly hit 85% on one node" usually is not; it fires often, rarely matters, and trains people to ignore alerts. Good alerts are tied to symptoms users feel, often expressed as how fast the error budget is being burned, and each one should come with a clear next step.

## Capacity and autoscaling

**Capacity planning** estimates the resources a system will need from expected load, growth, safety margins, and known bottlenecks, so it can meet its targets before it saturates. It asks questions such as: how much traffic will the holiday peak bring, which component runs out first, and how much headroom should remain if one zone is lost?

**Autoscaling** adjusts capacity automatically, changing the number or size of instances in response to measured demand or a schedule. It handles daily cycles and unexpected surges without keeping peak capacity running all the time.

A common mistake is to autoscale on CPU alone. Many services hit a different limit first: memory, queue depth, open connections, disk or network I/O, or a downstream service's rate limit. A queue worker waiting on a slow database may show low CPU while its backlog grows by the minute. Scaling on the signal that actually reflects saturation, such as queue length or requests in flight, works better.

## Multiple regions

A **multi-region deployment** runs a service in several geographic regions. It can reduce latency by serving users from a nearby region and can survive the outage of an entire region. The hard part is coordination: routing traffic between regions, and deciding how data is replicated across long distances where network latency makes synchronous coordination expensive.

## Disaster recovery targets

Two numbers define what "recovered" means after a disaster.

- **RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** is the maximum acceptable amount of recent data loss, measured backward from the failure. An RPO of 5 minutes means losing at most the last 5 minutes of writes, which rules out nightly backups as the only protection.
- **RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** is the target maximum time to restore an acceptable level of service after the disruption. An RTO of 1 hour means users should be served again within an hour.

Lower values for both cost more, through continuous replication, standby capacity, and automation.

## Testing recovery

A disaster recovery plan that has never been exercised is a hope, not a plan. Backups can be silently corrupt or incomplete, restore procedures can depend on a system that is itself down, and failover can take far longer than anyone assumed. Regular **disaster recovery tests** verify that data can actually be restored and that the process meets the RPO and RTO targets.

**Chaos experiments** extend this idea to everyday resilience. A team deliberately introduces a bounded failure, such as terminating instances, adding network latency, or blocking a dependency, and checks that timeouts, failover, and degradation behave as designed. Experiments start small, with a limited blast radius and a way to stop immediately, and grow as confidence does.

## Start from requirements

Everything in this deck is a trade-off, which is why good system design starts with the **workload and requirements**, not with favorite technologies. The key questions are:

1. What is the read-to-write ratio?
2. How much data is there, and how fast is it growing?
3. What latency do users need, and at which percentile?
4. What consistency does each operation require?
5. How much failure can the business tolerate, in availability, RPO, and RTO?

A read-heavy catalog, a write-heavy telemetry pipeline, and a payment ledger lead to very different architectures. Without those constraints, choosing a database, a cache, or a queue is mostly guessing.
