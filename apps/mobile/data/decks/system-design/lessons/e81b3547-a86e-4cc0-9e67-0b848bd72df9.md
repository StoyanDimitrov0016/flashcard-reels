# The CAP theorem and consensus

The previous lesson showed that strong consistency costs something during ordinary operation — coordination, latency. The CAP theorem names the moment that cost becomes unavoidable: a network partition, when some nodes cannot reach others at all. This lesson covers what CAP actually says, what a system does on either side of that choice, and how nodes agree on anything at all despite failures.

## What CAP actually says

The **CAP theorem** states that when a network partition prevents nodes from communicating, a distributed data system cannot guarantee both linearizable consistency and availability for every request. Precision matters here, because CAP is one of the most casually misquoted ideas in system design. It is not "pick two of Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance out of three, forever" — partitions happen whether or not you "choose" partition tolerance, and outside of a partition, a well-built system can offer both consistency and availability at once. CAP is specifically about the choice forced _during_ a partition, for the requests that a partition actually affects.

## Choosing consistency during a partition

**Choosing consistency** during a partition means some requests are rejected or delayed rather than returning a result that could violate the required consistency guarantee. A node cut off from the rest of the cluster, unable to confirm it holds the current, agreed-upon state, refuses to answer rather than risk answering with data that might already be wrong — the system stays correct at the cost of some requests failing outright while the partition lasts. Systems that make this choice are often labeled **CP**.

## Choosing availability during a partition

**Choosing availability** during a partition means nodes continue answering requests despite incomplete coordination, accepting that clients may observe conflicting or stale state that must later reconcile. A node cut off from the rest of the cluster keeps serving reads and writes from whatever state it locally has, rather than refusing — the system stays responsive at the cost of correctness guarantees weakening for the duration, and some mechanism (last-write-wins, version vectors, application-level merge logic) has to reconcile any conflicting writes once the partition heals. Systems that make this choice are often labeled **AP**.

Neither choice is universally correct — a payment ledger usually needs to lean CP (better to refuse a transfer than risk two conflicting balances), while a shopping cart or a social media like-count usually leans AP (better to keep working, slightly stale, than to go down).

## Quorums: a tunable middle ground

Rather than a fixed all-or-nothing choice, **quorum replication** lets reads and writes contact a configured number of replicas. Overlapping read and write quorums can increase the chance or guarantee, under assumptions, that reads encounter recent writes. Concretely, with `N` replicas, a write quorum of `W` and a read quorum of `R`: if `W + R > N`, every read quorum is guaranteed to overlap with every write quorum by at least one replica, so at least one replica in any read has seen the latest write — assuming no conflicting concurrent writes and correctly functioning replicas, hence "under assumptions." Systems built on quorums let operators tune `W` and `R` to lean more toward consistency (larger `W`, slower writes, stronger read guarantees) or more toward availability (smaller `W` and `R`, faster, more tolerant of unreachable replicas) without hard-coding the CP/AP choice for every operation.

## Split-brain

**Split-brain** is a specific, dangerous failure: separated nodes both believe they are authoritative and accept conflicting operations. It typically follows an availability-leaning failover: a leader appears unreachable, a follower is promoted to take over, but the original leader is actually still up (only network-partitioned, not crashed) and keeps accepting writes too — now two nodes both think they are "the" leader, and both are taking writes that the other does not know about. Leader election fencing (making sure a demoted or partitioned former leader can no longer take actions as leader, often by revoking its access) or quorum rules (requiring a majority of nodes to agree before any promotion) are used to prevent or contain it.

## Consensus

Coordinated decisions — who the leader is, what the next entry in a replicated log is — need nodes to agree despite some of them being unreachable or slow. A **consensus algorithm** lets multiple nodes agree on an ordered sequence or chosen value despite some failures, commonly for leader election and replicated state machines. Algorithms such as Raft and Paxos guarantee that as long as a majority of nodes are healthy and can communicate, the cluster agrees on one consistent answer — the same majority-based idea behind quorum reads and writes, applied to agreeing on cluster-wide facts like "who is the leader" rather than to an individual key's value.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting CAP as "you must always sacrifice one of the three," rather than as a specific trade-off that only bites during an actual network partition.
- Treating "AP" as a free pass to ignore conflicts. Choosing availability during a partition defers a consistency problem; something must still reconcile the divergent state once the partition heals.
- Building automatic failover without fencing, which is exactly the setup that produces split-brain: a promoted replica and a still-alive original leader, both accepting writes.

## Key points

- The CAP theorem: during a network partition, a distributed data system cannot guarantee both linearizable consistency and availability for every request.
- Choosing consistency (CP) rejects or delays requests during a partition rather than risk an incorrect answer; choosing availability (AP) keeps answering and reconciles conflicts later.
- Quorum replication (`W + R > N`) tunes the trade-off by requiring overlapping read and write quorums, rather than a fixed CP/AP choice.
- Split-brain is separated nodes each believing they are authoritative and accepting conflicting writes; fencing and quorum rules prevent or contain it.
- A consensus algorithm lets nodes agree on an ordered sequence or value despite failures, commonly used for leader election and replicated state machines.
