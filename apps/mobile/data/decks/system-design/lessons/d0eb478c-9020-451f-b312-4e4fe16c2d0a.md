# Multi-region deployment and disaster recovery

Everything covered so far in this deck — redundancy, replication, autoscaling — can still be defeated by one event large enough: an entire data center or cloud region going offline, from a power failure, a fiber cut, or a natural disaster. This lesson covers surviving that scale of failure, and, just as importantly, how you prove in advance that your recovery plan actually works.

## Multi-region deployment

**Multi-region deployment** runs a service in multiple geographic regions to reduce latency or survive a regional outage, while coordinating traffic and data across regions. Serving users from the region nearest them reduces network latency (an earlier motivation, similar to a CDN's edge locations); surviving a regional outage is the reliability motivation this lesson focuses on, since redundancy within a single region does nothing if the entire region — every availability zone within it — goes down together.

Multi-region is a substantial escalation in complexity, not a small configuration change: data now has to be replicated across regions (with the latency and consistency trade-offs from the replication and CAP lessons applying at a much larger physical distance, since a cross-region round trip is tens to over a hundred milliseconds, not the sub-millisecond latency within one data center), and traffic routing has to know how to redirect users away from a failed region, ideally before they notice anything is wrong.

## Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

Even a well-replicated multi-region system can lose some very recent writes if a region fails before they finished replicating elsewhere. **RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** is the maximum acceptable amount of recent data loss, measured backward from a failure. An RPO of 5 minutes means: if a failure happens right now, the business accepts losing up to the last 5 minutes of writes that had not yet replicated or been backed up elsewhere — anything older than that must be recoverable.

RPO is a business decision disguised as a technical one: a financial ledger might demand an RPO of zero (no acceptable data loss, which usually forces synchronous cross-region replication and its latency cost), while an analytics pipeline might tolerate an RPO of an hour (asynchronous replication or periodic batch backups are fine, since losing an hour of analytics events is a real but survivable cost).

## Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

Separately from how much data can be lost, there is the question of how long the outage itself can last. **RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** is the target maximum time to restore an acceptable level of service after a disruption. An RTO of 15 minutes means the plan must actually bring the service back within 15 minutes of a regional failure being detected — not "eventually," and not "once an engineer wakes up and looks at their laptop."

RPO and RTO together define what a disaster recovery plan actually has to deliver: how much can be lost, and how long can the recovery take. A plan that only addresses one of the two is incomplete — restoring service in 10 minutes with an RTO target of 15 is a good result only if the RPO target was also met, and vice versa.

## Why disaster recovery must be tested

**Disaster recovery should be tested** because backups and failover plans can silently fail or be too slow; exercises verify that data can actually be restored and that procedures meet RPO and RTO targets. A backup job that has been "succeeding" for two years, according to its own logs, but writing to a bucket nobody can actually read from during an emergency, is a real and common failure mode — and it is invisible until the day it is needed, unless someone deliberately tries a full restore beforehand and confirms it actually works, within the time the RTO allows.

## Chaos experiments

Waiting for a genuine multi-hour regional outage to discover that failover does not actually work is far too late. A **chaos experiment** deliberately introduces a bounded failure, such as killing instances or adding latency, to verify that resilience mechanisms behave as expected — under controlled conditions, on a schedule the team chooses, with the ability to stop the experiment if something goes seriously wrong. Killing a percentage of instances in one availability zone and confirming that load balancing, health checks, and autoscaling actually detect and route around the loss (rather than assuming they would) turns "we believe this is resilient" into "we tested that this is resilient" — for a small, deliberately bounded version of the failure a real outage would inflict at much larger and less controlled scale.

## Worked example

A company sets an RPO of 5 minutes and an RTO of 30 minutes for its primary region failing over to a secondary region.

1. Data is replicated asynchronously to the secondary region roughly every 60 seconds, comfortably within the 5-minute RPO even accounting for a spike in replication lag.
2. A quarterly chaos experiment simulates the primary region becoming unreachable and times the full failover: DNS and traffic routing updates, the secondary region's databases being promoted, and services in the secondary region confirmed healthy and serving real traffic.
3. The first attempt takes 47 minutes — past the 30-minute RTO — because the runbook's manual DNS update step took much longer than assumed. That gap is only discovered because the experiment was run; without it, the same delay would have played out during a genuine regional outage, with real users affected, and no one would have known the 30-minute RTO could not actually be met until it was too late to fix it.
4. The team automates the DNS step and reruns the experiment next quarter, now completing failover in 12 minutes.

## Common mistakes

- Setting an RPO and RTO on paper without ever testing whether the actual infrastructure and runbooks can meet them.
- Assuming backups are working because the backup job reports success, without periodically performing a real restore to confirm the data is actually recoverable and complete.
- Treating multi-region deployment as purely a latency optimization and skipping the harder work of actually validating cross-region failover, which is what makes it a reliability investment as well.

## Key points

- Multi-region deployment runs a service across geographic regions to reduce latency or survive a regional outage, requiring coordinated cross-region traffic routing and data replication.
- RPO is the maximum acceptable amount of recent data loss, measured backward from a failure.
- RTO is the target maximum time to restore an acceptable level of service after a disruption.
- Disaster recovery must be tested because backups and failover plans can silently fail or be too slow, and only a real exercise verifies that RPO and RTO targets are actually met.
- A chaos experiment deliberately introduces a bounded failure to verify that resilience mechanisms behave as expected, before a real, uncontrolled failure tests them instead.
