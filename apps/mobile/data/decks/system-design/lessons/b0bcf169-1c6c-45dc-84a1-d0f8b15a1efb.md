# Scale and performance

Every system design conversation eventually asks two questions: how fast is it, and what happens when more people use it? This lesson gives you the vocabulary for both. You will meet the two basic ways to grow a system and the two numbers that describe how well it performs while it grows.

## What scalability means

**Scalability** is a system's ability to handle more load when you add resources, without an unacceptable loss of latency, throughput, or reliability. The important words are "by adding resources." A system that only copes with growth after a rewrite is not scalable in this sense. A scalable one lets you respond to more users, more data, or more requests by adding hardware or instances while it keeps behaving acceptably.

Load can mean many things: requests per second, number of stored records, concurrent connections, or the size of each request. A design can scale well along one dimension and badly along another, so it helps to say which load you mean.

## Scaling up: vertical scaling

**Vertical scaling**, often called scaling up, means giving a single machine more CPU, more memory, or faster and larger storage. Its main attraction is simplicity. The application does not need to change, there is no data to split, and there is no coordination between machines.

It has two limits. First, hardware has a ceiling: at some point you cannot buy a bigger machine, and the largest machines are disproportionately expensive. Second, one large machine is one large **failure domain**. If it crashes or needs maintenance, everything it served goes down with it.

## Scaling out: horizontal scaling

**Horizontal scaling**, or scaling out, adds more machines or service instances and spreads the work across them. Ten modest servers behind a load balancer can serve more traffic than one, and if one fails the other nine keep going. Horizontal scaling therefore increases both capacity and **redundancy**.

The price is complexity. Work has to be distributed, which needs routing and **coordination**. Data that no longer fits on one machine has to be **partitioned** across several. Instances must agree on shared state or avoid holding it. Most large systems scale out in the end, but they pay for it with the distributed-systems problems covered in later lessons.

A common path is to scale up first, because it is cheap in engineering effort, and to scale out when the ceiling or the failure domain becomes a problem.

## Throughput and latency

Two measurements describe performance, and they are easy to confuse.

- **Throughput** is how much work the system completes per unit of time: requests per second, messages per second, or rows written per minute. It describes capacity.
- **Latency** is the elapsed time for one operation to complete, measured from the caller's point of view. It includes queuing, network time, and processing, not only the time the server spends computing.

The two are related but not the same. A system can have high throughput and poor latency, for example when it batches many requests together and makes each one wait. It can also have low latency at light load and then see latency climb sharply as it approaches its throughput limit, because requests start waiting in queues.

## Why percentiles beat averages

Latency is not one number; every request has its own. The tempting summary is the average, but averages hide the requests that matter most.

Imagine 100 requests where 98 take 20 ms and 2 take 3 seconds. The average is about 80 ms, which sounds fine. Yet two out of every hundred users waited three seconds, and for a busy service that is thousands of unhappy people.

**Percentiles** describe the distribution instead. The **p50** (median) is the latency that half of requests beat. The **p99** is the latency that 99% of requests beat, which exposes the slowest 1%, known as the **tail**. Engineers usually track p50, p95, and p99 together:

- p50 tells you the typical experience.
- p95 and p99 tell you how bad the slow cases are.

Tail latency matters more than it first appears. A single page may make many backend calls, and a user who touches the service many times a day is likely to hit the tail at least once. Percentiles make that group visible, while an average smooths it away.
