# Reliability metrics

"Make it reliable" is not an engineering target — it does not say how reliable, measured how, or what happens when the inevitable failure occurs. This lesson gives reliability a precise vocabulary: the properties a system can promise, and the numbers that turn "reliable" into something you can measure, put in a dashboard, and hold a team accountable to.

## Availability, reliability, and durability

These three words are often used loosely as synonyms. They are not the same guarantee.

**Availability** is the fraction of time or requests for which a service can successfully perform its intended function. Note the "or": availability is sometimes measured as uptime over a period (was the service up at all, at each moment) and sometimes as the fraction of requests that succeeded (of everything sent, how much got a good response). These can diverge — a service can be "up" while returning errors to a fraction of requests, which counts as unavailable under the request-based measure but not under the uptime measure. Whichever you use, define it precisely: "99.9% available" is meaningless without saying which one.

**Reliability** is broader: the ability to produce correct results consistently over time, including under expected failures and load. A service can be available (it responds) while being unreliable (the responses are sometimes wrong); reliability is about correctness holding up, not just about answering at all.

**Durability** is the guarantee that acknowledged data remains stored despite failures within the system's stated fault model. This is specifically about data, not about the service being reachable: a database can be temporarily unavailable (down for maintenance) while remaining perfectly durable (nothing already committed was lost), and vice versa: a service can be technically reachable while its storage is actively corrupting data.

## Turning "reliable" into a target: SLOs

A **service-level objective (SLO)** is a target for a measurable reliability indicator — for example, 99.9% of requests succeed within 300ms over a rolling 30-day window. The measurable indicator itself (percentage of successful requests, or of requests under a latency threshold) is usually called a service-level indicator (SLI); the SLO is the number you promise for it.

Concretely: "99.9%" sounds close to 100%, but multiplied against real traffic it is a real, calculable allowance for failure. At one million requests per day, 99.9% allows 1,000 failed requests daily. At 99.99%, that allowance drops to 100. Going from three nines to four nines is not a 0.09 percentage-point improvement in difficulty — it is a tenfold reduction in the number of things allowed to go wrong, which usually means qualitatively different engineering (removing more single points of failure, more automation, faster detection).

## Spending reliability deliberately: error budgets

If the SLO is 99.9% successful requests, the remaining 0.1% is not a rounding error to be ashamed of — it is a resource. An **error budget** is the amount of unreliability allowed by an SLO. Teams can spend it on changes — shipping features, running risky migrations, rolling out experiments — while slowing releases when reliability falls too far and the budget is nearly exhausted.

This reframes reliability work from "always chase zero failures" (impossible, and if pursued literally, it stalls all forward progress) to "stay within the budget we agreed to." A team that has burned through this month's error budget from a bad deploy should freeze further risky changes until the budget recovers; a team with budget to spare can afford to ship faster. The budget turns an abstract reliability goal into an operational decision rule that both engineers and their stakeholders can point to.

## Worked example

Suppose an API has an SLO of 99.95% successful requests per 30-day window, and serves 2,000,000 requests per day (60,000,000 per month).

- Allowed failures per month: 0.05% of 60,000,000 = 30,000 requests.
- A single bad deploy that causes a 10-minute outage at full traffic (2,000,000 / 1440 minutes ≈ 1,389 requests/minute) burns roughly 13,900 requests — nearly half the month's entire error budget in ten minutes.
- After that incident, the team has about 16,100 requests of budget left for the rest of the month: room for one more small incident, not for a second ten-minute outage.

This is the concrete mechanism behind "slow down after an incident": the budget's arithmetic, not just caution, says so.

## Key points

- Availability is the fraction of time or requests for which a service performs its function; it can be measured as uptime or as request success rate, and these are not interchangeable.
- Reliability is broader than availability: producing correct results consistently, including under failure and load.
- Durability is a guarantee about acknowledged data surviving failures within a stated fault model, distinct from the service being reachable.
- An SLO is a target for a measurable reliability indicator, such as 99.9% successful requests under a latency threshold over a defined period.
- Moving from 99.9% to 99.99% cuts the allowed failure count by 10x, not by a small percentage — each added "nine" is a much harder target.
- An error budget is the unreliability an SLO allows; teams spend it on risky changes and slow down when it runs low.
