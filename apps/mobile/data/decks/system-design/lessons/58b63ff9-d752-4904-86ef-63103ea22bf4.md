# Resilient service calls

Once a system is made of multiple services calling each other over a network, every call can fail in a way an in-process function call never could: the network can drop the request, the callee can be slow, overloaded, or down, and the caller might wait forever for a response that will never come. This lesson covers the patterns that keep one struggling dependency from taking down everything that calls it.

## Timeouts: bounding the wait

Without a timeout, a caller may wait indefinitely for a failed or partitioned dependency. **Timeouts** are necessary in distributed calls because a bound is needed on how long a caller waits: a timeout bounds resource occupancy (the thread, connection, or memory held while waiting) and lets recovery logic run — retry, fall back, or return an error — instead of hanging forever. A caller with no timeout, waiting on a dependency that will genuinely never respond, is itself becoming unavailable, one held resource at a time, for a failure that was not even its own.

## Retries and the storm they can cause

A timed-out or failed call is often retried. But **retries can make an outage worse**: many clients retrying a struggling dependency can multiply load exactly when capacity is lowest, causing a retry storm — the dependency that was merely slow, and might have recovered on its own, is now also hit with several times its normal traffic from every caller retrying at once, which can turn a temporary slowdown into a full outage.

Two techniques make retries safer. **Exponential backoff**: retry delays grow after successive failures (1s, 2s, 4s, 8s...), reducing pressure on a dependency that may need time to recover, rather than hammering it again immediately. **Jitter** on top of that backoff: randomness prevents many clients from retrying at the same synchronized times and creating repeated traffic spikes — without jitter, every client that failed at the same moment backs off by the same schedule and retries at the same moment again, recreating the exact spike, just delayed and repeated.

```text
attempt 1: fails at t=0
attempt 2: wait 1s + random(0, 0.5s), retry
attempt 3: wait 2s + random(0, 1s), retry
attempt 4: wait 4s + random(0, 2s), retry
```

## Circuit breakers: stop calling a broken dependency

Backoff still means every caller eventually tries again. A **circuit breaker** goes further: it stops sending normal requests to a repeatedly failing dependency for a period, failing fast while allowing controlled probes for recovery. Modeled on an electrical circuit breaker, it has three states: **closed** (requests flow normally, failures are counted), **open** (after too many failures, requests fail immediately without even attempting the call, sparing both the caller and the struggling dependency further load), and **half-open** (after a cooldown, a small number of probe requests are let through to check whether the dependency has recovered — if they succeed, the breaker closes again; if they fail, it reopens).

The benefit over plain timeouts and retries is speed and protection: a caller behind an open circuit breaker fails in microseconds instead of waiting out a timeout on every single request, and the struggling dependency stops receiving load from that caller entirely, giving it room to recover instead of being kept down by traffic that was never going to succeed anyway.

## Bulkheads: contain the blast radius

Even with timeouts and circuit breakers per dependency, one dependency's problems can still spread if it shares a resource pool with everything else. A **bulkhead** isolates resource pools or failure domains so overload in one dependency or workload cannot consume every thread, connection, or worker available to others — named for the watertight compartments in a ship's hull that keep one breach from flooding the whole vessel. A service calling five downstream dependencies from one shared thread pool can have that entire pool exhausted by one slow dependency, starving requests to the other four healthy ones; separate pools per dependency mean a slow dependency only exhausts its own allotment.

A bulkhead is often combined with the other two patterns in this lesson: a circuit breaker decides _whether_ to call a dependency at all, and a bulkhead limits how much of the service's own resources that dependency — failing or not — is allowed to occupy while it is being called. (A later lesson, on fan-out, covers the companion problem of coordinating one shared timeout deadline across several such calls made in parallel.)

## Common mistakes

- Retrying without backoff or jitter, especially on a shared failure — this is precisely how a slow dependency gets turned into a fully down one.
- Setting circuit breaker thresholds so sensitive that ordinary blips open the breaker constantly, which just relocates the instability rather than fixing it.
- Sharing one resource pool across every downstream dependency, so a single slow one can exhaust capacity that healthy dependencies also needed.

## Key points

- Timeouts bound how long a caller waits for a dependency, so a stuck call cannot occupy resources indefinitely.
- Uncoordinated retries during an outage can create a retry storm that overwhelms an already-struggling dependency.
- Exponential backoff grows retry delays after failures; jitter randomizes them so synchronized clients do not recreate the same spike.
- A circuit breaker stops calling a repeatedly failing dependency, failing fast, and periodically probes for recovery before resuming normal traffic.
- A bulkhead isolates resource pools per dependency or workload, so one dependency's overload cannot exhaust resources shared by others.
