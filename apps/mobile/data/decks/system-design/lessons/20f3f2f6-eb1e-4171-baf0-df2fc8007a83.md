# Synchronous and event-driven communication

Every pattern so far in this deck — timeouts, circuit breakers, fan-out — assumed a caller waiting on a response. That is one way services can talk to each other, but not the only one, and the choice between them shapes an architecture more than almost any other decision: it determines what "coupled" even means between two services.

## Synchronous communication

**Synchronous communication** means the caller waits for a downstream response before completing its own operation, creating direct latency and availability dependencies. An order service that calls the inventory service synchronously to check stock, and waits for that call's response before telling the customer whether the order can proceed, has made its own success directly dependent on the inventory service being up and reasonably fast right now. Every resilience pattern from the last two lessons — timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, bulkheads — exists specifically because synchronous calls create this direct dependency, and something has to bound how badly a struggling downstream service can hurt the caller.

Synchronous calls are the natural default because they are easy to reason about: the caller knows the outcome (success, failure, or timeout) before moving on, in program order, exactly like a normal function call.

## Event-driven architecture

**Event-driven architecture** takes a different approach: components publish events describing completed facts, and other components react asynchronously without the publisher calling each consumer directly. When the order service finishes creating an order, instead of directly calling every service that might care (billing, shipping, analytics, fraud detection), it publishes an `OrderCreated` event. Each interested service subscribes to that event and reacts on its own schedule. The order service does not know or care who is listening, or how many services are — new consumers can be added later without the publisher changing at all.

```text
synchronous:
  order service --calls--> inventory service --calls--> ...
  (order service waits, and depends, on every call succeeding)

event-driven:
  order service --publishes OrderCreated--> [event log / queue]
  billing service, shipping service, analytics --each subscribe and react independently
  (order service does not wait for any of them)
```

## The trade-off

Event-driven architecture reduces direct coupling — the order service does not fail, or even slow down, if the analytics consumer is temporarily behind — but it makes causality, retries, ordering, schema evolution, and end-to-end debugging more complex. "Reduces direct coupling" is a real benefit, not a slogan: the earlier lesson on queues showed exactly how the order service is insulated from a consumer being down or slow. The cost is that understanding what happened to one order now means tracing through several independently-reacting consumers instead of reading one linear call stack, and every delivery-guarantee, ordering, and idempotency concern from the queues lesson now applies to every one of those event consumers.

## Choosing between them

The two are not mutually exclusive within one system — most real architectures use synchronous calls where an immediate answer is required by the business logic (checking whether an item is in stock before confirming an order) and events where the answer can be produced later and by someone else (sending a receipt email, updating analytics, notifying a fraud model). The dividing question is usually: does the caller need to know the outcome before it can finish its own work? If yes, synchronous; if the outcome can be handled independently, whenever it becomes available, event-driven usually decouples the systems better without costing anything the caller actually needed.

## Common mistakes

- Making every downstream call synchronous by default, so a slow or down non-critical dependency (like analytics) can still fail or slow the entire request, when it was never actually on the critical path.
- Switching to event-driven architecture without also adopting the delivery-guarantee and idempotency discipline events require, and then being surprised by duplicate or out-of-order side effects in a consumer.
- Treating event-driven as strictly "more advanced" or "better" and using it even where a caller genuinely needs the outcome before proceeding — that case is what synchronous calls are for.

## Key points

- Synchronous communication makes the caller wait for a downstream response, creating direct latency and availability dependencies on that call succeeding.
- Event-driven architecture has components publish events describing completed facts, letting other components react asynchronously without the publisher calling them directly.
- Event-driven architecture reduces direct coupling between publisher and consumers, at the cost of harder causality tracing, retries, ordering, schema evolution, and debugging.
- Choose synchronous calls when the caller needs the outcome to finish its own work, and events when the outcome can be handled independently and later.
