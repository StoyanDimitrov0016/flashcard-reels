# Storage devices

RAM forgets everything when the power goes off, so anything that must survive, from databases to your source code, ends up on a storage device. Storage is also the slowest part of the memory hierarchy by a wide margin, and its performance depends heavily on how you access it. The same amount of data can take half a second or four minutes to read depending on the pattern. The physics are different for spinning disks and flash, and each rewards different habits, which is why so much database and filesystem design is shaped by these devices.

## Hard disk drives

A **hard disk drive (HDD)** stores data magnetically on spinning platters. A read/write head on a moving arm floats over the surface. To access a given block, the drive must:

1. **Seek**: move the arm to the right track, typically 4 to 9 ms.
2. **Wait for rotation**: wait until the block spins under the head. At 7,200 RPM a revolution takes 8.3 ms, so the average wait is about 4.2 ms.
3. **Transfer** the data, which for a 4 KB block takes only microseconds.

Positioning dominates. A random 4 KB read costs around 10 ms, which caps a disk at roughly 100 to 200 random operations per second.

## Why sequential access wins on HDDs

**Sequential accesses are faster than random accesses on HDDs** because a mechanical disk must move the head and wait for platter rotation to reach each random location, while sequential blocks can be transferred with far less repositioning. Once the head is in place, consecutive blocks stream past it at the full media rate, often 150 to 250 MB/s.

A worked example: read 100 MB from a 7,200 RPM disk.

```text
sequential:  100 MB / 200 MB/s                        ≈ 0.5 s
random 4 KB: 100 MB / 4 KB = 25,600 reads x ~10 ms     ≈ 256 s
```

Same data, roughly 500 times slower. This is why filesystems try to place a file's blocks contiguously, why databases favor large sequential scans and append-only logs on spinning disks, and why defragmentation used to matter.

## Solid-state drives

A **solid-state drive (SSD)** stores persistent data in flash memory. With no moving parts, there is no seek and no rotation: an NVMe SSD serves a random 4 KB read in tens of microseconds, around a hundred times faster than a hard disk, and can sustain hundreds of thousands of random reads per second.

Flash has a cost the disk does not: **finite write endurance**. Each flash cell survives only a limited number of program and erase cycles before it wears out, from a few thousand for common TLC flash down to around a thousand for denser QLC. Drives spread writes evenly across all cells (wear leveling) and are rated for a total amount of data written, such as 600 TB written or "1 drive write per day" for five years. On Linux, `smartctl -a /dev/nvme0` reports how much of that endurance a drive has used.

## Why SSD performance still depends on access pattern

SSDs remove mechanical delays, but **SSD performance still depends on access pattern**, because of how flash is written. Flash writes occur in pages but erasure happens in larger blocks, and controller garbage collection, queue depth, and write amplification all affect latency and throughput.

- A **flash page**, typically 4 to 16 KB, is the unit of reading and writing.
- A page cannot be overwritten in place. It must be erased first, and erasure works only on a whole **erase block** of hundreds of pages, several megabytes.

So the SSD controller never updates data in place. It writes the new version to a fresh, already-erased page, updates its internal map from logical block addresses to physical pages (the flash translation layer), and marks the old page stale. Eventually it runs out of fresh pages and must perform **garbage collection**: pick a block, copy its still-valid pages elsewhere, and erase the block.

That background work interferes with foreground requests. Small random overwrites scattered across many blocks leave blocks partially valid and make garbage collection expensive, so sustained random-write performance on a full drive can drop far below the advertised figure. Large sequential writes tend to fill and invalidate whole blocks together, which is cheap to clean.

**Queue depth**, the number of requests outstanding at once, matters too. An SSD is internally many flash chips working in parallel, and NVMe exposes many deep queues to use them. A program that issues one 4 KB read and waits for it before issuing the next leaves most of that parallelism idle; a program with 32 reads in flight can get many times the throughput.

## Write amplification

**Write amplification** is when the storage device physically writes more data than the application logically requested, often because existing flash blocks must be copied and erased during updates.

A worked example: an erase block holds 256 pages, and 200 of them still hold valid data. To reclaim this block, the controller copies the 200 valid pages to another block, erases, and gains 56 free pages. Filling those 56 pages with new host data costs 56 + 200 = 256 page writes, a write amplification factor of about 4.6. Every host write is costing almost five physical writes.

High write amplification reduces throughput and wears out the flash faster. It is reduced by:

- **Sequential, large writes**, which invalidate whole blocks together. Log-structured designs such as LSM-tree databases exploit this.
- **TRIM**, which tells the drive which blocks the filesystem no longer uses so it does not copy dead data. On Linux, `fstrim` or the `discard` mount option issue it.
- **Free space**: an SSD that is kept well below full, plus the drive's hidden overprovisioned capacity, gives garbage collection blocks with fewer valid pages to copy.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming access patterns stop mattering on SSDs. Random small writes, full drives, and low queue depth all still hurt.
- Comparing drives only by peak sequential throughput. Random-I/O latency and sustained write performance often matter more.
- Forgetting that SSD endurance is finite for write-heavy workloads such as logging or caching.

## Key points

- HDDs must seek and wait for rotation for each random location, so sequential access, which needs little repositioning, is far faster.
- An SSD stores data in flash memory, with much lower random-access latency than mechanical disks but finite write endurance.
- SSD performance still depends on access pattern: flash writes in pages but erases in larger blocks, and garbage collection, queue depth, and write amplification affect latency and throughput.
- Write amplification is when the device physically writes more than the application requested, often because valid data must be copied before blocks are erased.
