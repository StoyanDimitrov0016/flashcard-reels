# Joins

A well-designed relational schema splits data across tables: customers in one, orders in another, products in a third. That keeps each fact in one place, but almost every useful question crosses those boundaries. "Show each order with the customer's name" needs data from two tables at once. Joins are how SQL puts related rows back together, and knowing exactly which rows each join produces is the difference between a correct report and one that silently drops or double-counts data.

## The example data

```text
customers                orders
 id | name               id | customer_id | total
----+-------             ----+-------------+-------
  1 | Ada                 10 |           1 | 25.00
  2 | Grace               11 |           1 | 12.50
  3 | Linus               12 |           2 | 40.00
```

Ada has two orders, Grace has one, and Linus has none.

## Inner join

An **inner join** returns the row combinations for which the join condition matches on both sides:

```sql
SELECT c.name, o.id AS order_id, o.total
FROM customers AS c
INNER JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id;
```

```text
 name  | order_id | total
-------+----------+-------
 Ada   |       10 | 25.00
 Ada   |       11 | 12.50
 Grace |       12 | 40.00
```

Linus does not appear: no order matches him. An order with no matching customer would not appear either. Inner joins keep only matches, which is why `INNER JOIN` is often written simply as `JOIN`.

## Left join

A **left join** (`LEFT OUTER JOIN`) returns every row from the left side, plus matching rows from the right side. When no right row matches, the right-side columns are filled with null:

```sql
SELECT c.name, o.id AS order_id, o.total
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id;
```

```text
 name  | order_id | total
-------+----------+-------
 Ada   |       10 | 25.00
 Ada   |       11 | 12.50
 Grace |       12 | 40.00
 Linus |     null |  null
```

Left joins answer questions where the absence of a match matters: "all customers, with their orders if any". They also make a common pattern possible, finding rows with no match:

```sql
SELECT c.name
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE o.id IS NULL;      -- customers who never ordered: Linus
```

Be careful where you put conditions on the right table. `WHERE o.total > 20` runs after the join and removes the null-extended rows, quietly turning the left join back into an inner join. To keep every customer while matching only large orders, put the condition in the `ON` clause: `ON o.customer_id = c.id AND o.total > 20`.

A **right join** is the mirror image, keeping every row from the right side, and a **full outer join** keeps unmatched rows from both sides.

## Cross join

A **cross join** returns every combination of a row from the left input with a row from the right input, with no join condition at all. This is the **Cartesian product**: 3 customers crossed with 3 orders produce 9 rows.

```sql
SELECT s.size, c.color
FROM sizes AS s
CROSS JOIN colors AS c;   -- every size in every color
```

Cross joins are useful for generating combinations, such as every product variant or every day of a calendar for every store. They are dangerous by accident: listing two tables in `FROM` without a join condition produces a cross join, and 10,000 rows crossed with 10,000 rows is 100 million.

## Why joins multiply rows

A join produces **one output row per matching combination**. If one row on the left matches several rows on the right, that left row appears several times in the result. You saw this with Ada: one customer row, two orders, two output rows.

That is correct and intended, but it surprises people when aggregating. Suppose each order also has several payments and several shipments:

```sql
SELECT o.id, SUM(p.amount) AS paid
FROM orders AS o
JOIN payments  AS p ON p.order_id = o.id
JOIN shipments AS s ON s.order_id = o.id
GROUP BY o.id;
```

If order 10 has two payments and three shipments, the joins produce 2 × 3 = 6 rows for that order. Each payment appears three times, and `SUM(p.amount)` reports three times the real amount. Nothing errors; the number is just wrong.

The fix is to aggregate each one-to-many relationship separately before combining them, for example in subqueries or CTEs (covered in the next lesson), or to check whether you need the second join at all. Whenever a total looks suspiciously large, count the rows each join produces.

## Common mistakes

- **Using an inner join when unmatched rows matter.** Customers with no orders vanish from the report.
- **Filtering the right table of a left join in `WHERE`.** It removes the null rows and behaves like an inner join.
- **Forgetting a join condition.** You get a cross join and a row explosion.
- **Aggregating across two one-to-many joins.** Row multiplication inflates sums and counts.

## Key points

- An inner join returns row combinations for which the join condition matches on both sides.
- A left join returns every row from the left side and matching right rows; when no right row matches, right-side columns are null.
- A cross join returns every combination of a left row with a right row.
- If one row matches multiple rows on the other side, the result contains one output row per matching combination.
- Row multiplication from joins can silently inflate aggregates such as `SUM` and `COUNT`.
